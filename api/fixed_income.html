financialkit.fixed_income#

financialkit.fixed_income hub · History · Examples · API reference

Bond cash flows, prices and yield to maturity (scipy.optimize.brentq), accrued interest and STRIPS; Macaulay, modified and Fisher-Weil duration, convexity and key rate durations; Lutz forward rates; zero curves bootstrapped from bond prices or par rates, Carleton-Cooper discount factors and McCulloch’s spline discount function (numpy.linalg.lstsq); Nelson-Siegel and Svensson fits (scipy.optimize.least_squares) and the principal components of curve moves (numpy.linalg.eigh); Redington immunization and the Fong-Vasicek \(M^2\) bound; and interest-rate and currency swaps.

financialkit.fixed_income: bonds, duration, yield curves, immunization and swaps.

Bond cash flows, prices and yield to maturity (scipy.optimize.brentq()), accrued interest and STRIPS; Macaulay, modified and Fisher-Weil duration, convexity and Ho’s key rate durations; Lutz forward rates, bootstrapping a zero curve from bond prices or par rates (hand-written, solving each node with brentq()), Carleton-Cooper discount factors and McCulloch’s spline discount function (numpy.linalg.lstsq()), Nelson-Siegel and Svensson fits (scipy.optimize.least_squares()) and the principal components of curve moves (numpy.linalg.eigh()); Redington immunization and the Fong-Vasicek \(M^2\) bound; and interest-rate and currency swaps valued as pairs of bonds.

Every curve is a YieldCurve of continuously compounded zero rates, and every function that takes a curve also accepts a flat rate. Times are year fractions; converting dates under a day-count convention belongs to financialkit.time_value.

class financialkit.fixed_income.NelsonSiegelCurve(beta0, beta1, beta2, tau)[source]#

Bases: YieldCurve

Nelson and Siegel’s (1987) parsimonious yield curve.

\[r(t) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \frac{1 - e^{-t/\tau}}{t/\tau} + \beta_2 \left(\frac{1 - e^{-t/\tau}}{t/\tau} - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\]

\(\beta_0\) is the long rate, \(\beta_0 + \beta_1\) the short rate, \(\beta_2\) a hump whose position \(\tau\) sets.

Examples

>>> curve = NelsonSiegelCurve(0.05, -0.02, 0.01, 2.0)
>>> round(float(curve.zero_rate(0.0)), 6), round(float(curve.zero_rate(1e6)), 6)
(0.03, 0.05)

financialkit.fixed_income.bootstrap_zero_curve(maturities, coupon_rates, prices, freq=2, face=100.0)[source]#

Strip a zero curve from coupon bond prices, shortest maturity first.

Each bond adds one node at its maturity. Its earlier cash flows are discounted on the curve built so far, linearly interpolated in the zero rate; when a coupon falls between the last known node and the new one, its rate depends on the unknown node too, so the node is solved for with scipy.optimize.brentq() rather than by back-substitution.

Parameters:

  • maturities (array_like, shape (n,)) – Distinct bond maturities (years).

  • coupon_rates (array_like, shape (n,)) – Annual coupon rates (0 for zero-coupon bonds).

  • prices (array_like, shape (n,)) – Dirty prices per face.

  • freq (int) – Coupon frequency.

  • face (float)

Return type:

ZeroCurve

Returns:

ZeroCurve – Nodes at the bond maturities.

Examples

Hull (9th ed., Tables 4.3-4.4):

>>> curve = bootstrap_zero_curve([0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0], [0, 0, 0, 0.08, 0.12], [97.5, 94.9, 90.0, 96.0, 101.6])
>>> (100 * curve.rates).round(3).tolist()

financialkit.fixed_income.carleton_cooper_discount_factors(times, cash_flow_matrix, prices)[source]#

Discount factors by regressing bond prices on their cash flows.

Bond \(i\) pays \(C_{ik}\) at the common dates \(t_k\), so \(P_i = \sum_k C_{ik} d_k\). With as many bonds as dates this is a triangular system (bootstrapping); with more bonds it is solved in the least-squares sense, which averages out pricing noise.

Parameters:

  • times (array_like, shape (m,)) – Payment dates shared by the bonds.

  • cash_flow_matrix (array_like, shape (n, m)) – Row i holds bond i’s payment on each date (0 where none).

  • prices (array_like, shape (n,))

Return type:

CurveFitResult

Returns:

CurveFitResult – params are the discount factors; curve the implied zero curve.

Examples

>>> res = carleton_cooper_discount_factors([1.0, 2.0], [[105.0, 0.0], [6.0, 106.0]], [100.0, 99.0])
>>> res.params.round(6).tolist()

financialkit.fixed_income.fisher_weil_duration(cash_flows, curve)[source]#

Fisher-Weil duration: Macaulay’s weights computed on a zero curve.

\[D_{FW} = \frac{1}{P} \sum_i t_i \, c_i \, P(0, t_i), \qquad P = \sum_i c_i \, P(0, t_i)\]

It is exactly \(-\frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{ds}\) for a parallel shift \(s\) of the continuously compounded zero curve.

>>> from financialkit.fixed_income.systems.bonds import bond_cash_flows
>>> cf = bond_cash_flows(100.0, 0.05, 3.0, freq=1)
>>> round(fisher_weil_duration(cf, ZeroCurve([1, 2, 3], [0.02, 0.03, 0.04])), 4)
2.8588

Fit McCulloch’s spline discount function to bond prices by linear least squares.

Because the discount function is linear in its coefficients, so is every bond price: \(P_i - \sum_k c_{ik} = \sum_j a_j \sum_k c_{ik} g_j(t_{ik})\).

Parameters:
