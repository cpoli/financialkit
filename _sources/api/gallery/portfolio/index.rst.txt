Examples
========

This gallery walks through every public feature of ``financialkit.portfolio``:
Markowitz's frontier, Tobin's tangency portfolio, Merton's analytic
frontier and the 1/N benchmark; Kelly betting, Merton's portfolio problem
and CPPI; single-index and Ledoit-Wolf covariance estimates; the Sharpe
and Sortino ratios and Grinold's fundamental law; Black-Litterman and
Michaud resampling; and CVaR optimization, maximum diversification, risk
parity and hierarchical risk parity.

Each script in this gallery is self-contained and can be run directly with
``python examples/portfolio/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **mean_variance** -- the Markowitz frontier, Tobin separation, Merton's
  closed form and DeMiguel-Garlappi-Uppal's 1/N.
- **growth** -- the Kelly criterion, Merton's portfolio problem and CPPI.
- **covariance** -- Sharpe's single-index model and Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage.
- **performance** -- the Sharpe ratio, Grinold's fundamental law and the
  Sortino ratio.
- **bayesian** -- Black-Litterman and Michaud's resampled frontier.
- **risk_based** -- CVaR optimization, the most-diversified portfolio,
  risk parity and hierarchical risk parity.

Estimation-aware optimization
-----------------------------

Taming the error in expected returns with a market prior or by
resampling.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Fischer Black and Robert Litterman (1992), at Goldman Sachs, started mean-variance optimization from the expected returns that make the market portfolio optimal, \Pi = \delta\Sigma w_{mkt}, rather than from historical averages. Investors&#x27; views then tilt those returns in proportion to their confidence. This reproduces He and Litterman&#x27;s (1999) seven-country example: the view that German equities will beat the rest of Europe by 5% moves weight into Germany, out of France and the UK, and leaves the other countries&#x27; weights alone.">

Covariance estimation
---------------------

Structured and shrunk estimates of the covariance matrix, the input that
mean-variance optimizers are most sensitive to after the means.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Markowitz&#x27;s method needed every pairwise covariance, d(d+1)/2 numbers, which no 1960s computer (or analyst) could supply for hundreds of stocks. William Sharpe (1963) assumed that stocks move together only through a common market index, R_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_m + \varepsilon_i, so the covariance is \sigma_m^2\beta\beta&#x27; + \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^2), with 3d + 1 parameters. The structure also filters noise: with short histories, the single-index estimate is closer to the truth than the sample covariance.">

.. only:: html

  .. image:: /api/gallery/portfolio/covariance/images/thumb/sphx_glr_plot_01_single_index_thumb.png
    :alt:

      <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnail-title">Sharpe's single-index model</div>
    </div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="When the number of assets is comparable to the number of observations, the sample covariance&#x27;s eigenvalues spread out far more than the true ones: the smallest are too small, so the minimum-variance optimizer loads up on spurious &quot;riskless&quot; combinations. Olivier Ledoit and Michael Wolf (2004) shrank the sample matrix towards a structured target, \delta F + (1 - \delta)S, with the intensity \delta estimated from the data to minimize the expected squared error. Their two targets are a scaled identity and the constant-correlation matrix.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="John Kelly (1956), at Bell Labs, asked how a gambler with a private wire should bet. Staking the fraction f of wealth on a bet won with probability p at odds b, wealth grows at the rate g(f) = p\log(1 + bf) + q\log(1 - f) per bet, maximized by f^* = p - q/b. The Kelly bettor eventually outgrows any other fixed-fraction strategy; betting more than twice Kelly makes growth negative even though every bet is favourable.">

Mean-variance
-------------

Portfolios with the least variance for their mean, with and without
short sales and a riskless asset, and the naive benchmark they struggle
to beat out of sample.

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Harry Markowitz (1952) argued that an investor should look at a portfolio&#x27;s expected return and its variance together, and hold only portfolios with the least variance for their mean. Diversification then has a precise meaning: combining imperfectly correlated assets moves the frontier to the left of every asset on its own. With short sales the frontier is a hyperbola; without them, every point is a quadratic program, solved here with scipy.optimize.minimize.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="James Tobin (1958) added a riskless asset to Markowitz&#x27;s problem. Combinations of cash and any risky portfolio lie on a straight line, and the steepest such line touches the frontier at the tangency portfolio. Every mean-variance investor holds that same mix of risky assets and differs only in how much cash goes with it: the risky choice separates from the attitude to risk. The line is the capital market line, and its slope is the highest attainable Sharpe ratio.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="William Sharpe (1966) ranked 34 mutual funds by their reward-to-variability ratio, the mean excess return divided by its standard deviation. By Tobin&#x27;s separation it is the right yardstick for an investor&#x27;s whole portfolio: leverage changes the return but not the ratio. It is also an estimate, and Andrew Lo (2002) showed how noisy: with independent returns its standard error is \sqrt{(1 + SR^2/2)/n} per period, so years of data separate good managers from lucky ones only slowly.">
