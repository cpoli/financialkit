Examples
========

This gallery walks through every public feature of ``financialkit.time_series``:
the stylized facts of returns, tests of the random walk, ARMA models and
order selection, unit roots and cointegration, long memory, and
conditional-volatility models. Each script illustrates one dated
breakthrough from the :doc:`history page </history/time_series_breakthroughs>`.
All data are synthetic and seeded; no market data is downloaded.

Each script in this gallery is self-contained and can be run directly with
``python examples/time_series/<section>/<script>.py``.

Sections
--------

- **linear_models** -- Yule's autoregression, Box-Jenkins ARMA and
  Akaike's information criterion.
- **long_memory** -- Hurst's rescaled range.
- **efficiency** -- Kendall's serial correlations, Fama's runs test, the
  Ljung-Box portmanteau test and Lo-MacKinlay variance ratios.
- **stylized_facts** -- Mandelbrot's fat tails and the Hill tail index.
- **unit_roots** -- the Dickey-Fuller test and Engle-Granger cointegration.
- **volatility** -- Parkinson's range, ARCH, GARCH, EGARCH, GJR-GARCH,
  RiskMetrics EWMA and realized volatility.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="To size the Aswan reservoirs, Harold Edwin Hurst (1951) studied the range of cumulative departures of the Nile&#x27;s annual flow from its mean: the storage a dam would need to deliver the average flow every year. Divided by the standard deviation, the range of independent inflows grows like n^{1/2}; Hurst found n^{0.73} for the Nile and many other records. Wet years cluster with wet years. Mandelbrot later linked the exponent H to fractional Brownian motion: H &gt; 1/2 means persistence, H &lt; 1/2 mean reversion, and in markets a reliable H \ne 1/2 would make returns predictable.">

Unit roots and cointegration
----------------------------

The Dickey-Fuller distribution and the Engle-Granger two-step test.


.. raw:: html

  <div id='sg-tag-list' class='sphx-glr-tag-list'></div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnails">

.. thumbnail-parent-div-open

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Is a series a random walk (\rho = 1) or does it revert to a mean (:math:`\rho &lt; 1`)? Regressing \Delta y_t on y_{t-1} and looking at the t-ratio of the slope seems natural, but under a unit root that ratio does not have a t distribution: it is skewed to the left, and more so when a constant or trend is included. David Dickey and Wayne Fuller (1979) tabulated its distribution by simulation. Their test, with lagged differences added (Said and Dickey 1984) and MacKinnon&#x27;s critical values, is still the first thing run on an interest rate, a price-dividend ratio or a pairs-trading spread.">

.. only:: html

  .. image:: /api/gallery/time_series/unit_roots/images/thumb/sphx_glr_plot_01_dickey_fuller_thumb.png
    :alt:

  :doc:`/api/gallery/time_series/unit_roots/plot_01_dickey_fuller`

.. raw:: html

      <div class="sphx-glr-thumbnail-title">Dickey-Fuller unit roots</div>
    </div>


.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Granger and Newbold (1974) showed that regressing one random walk on an independent one gives &quot;significant&quot; slopes and high R^2 most of the time: a spurious regression. Yet some nonstationary series are tied together, such as spot and futures prices, or two share classes of the same company. Robert Engle and Clive Granger (1987) called them cointegrated: some combination y_t - b x_t is stationary. Their two-step test regresses y on x and runs a Dickey-Fuller test on the residuals, with critical values that allow for b having been fitted. It is the textbook basis of pairs trading.">
